1. CARTER & DIAZ, "THE ELEMENTS OF POP-UP" (1999) — TAXONOMY
===================================================================

VERIFIED (via direct quotation in a peer-reviewed paper that cites the book, cross-checked against a second museum source that independently attributes the same framework):

Carter & Diaz categorize pop-up mechanisms into exactly four families:
- Parallel folds
- Angle folds
- Wheels (volvelles)
- Pull tabs

Source: Winder, B.G., Magleby, S.P., Howell, L.L., "Kinematic Representations of Pop-Up Paper Mechanisms," ASME J. Mechanisms and Robotics, Vol. 1, 021009, May 2009 (originally presented at ASME IDETC/CIE 2007), which states verbatim: "Carter and Diaz [1] categorized mechanisms as parallel folds, angle folds, wheels, and pull tabs." This is independently corroborated by the Robert C. Williams Museum of Papermaking's "Movable Mechanisms" exhibit page, which credits the same four-category framework explicitly to "The Elements of Pop-Up by James Diaz and David A. Carter" and maps them to two behavioral classes: pull tabs/wheels (user-actuated) vs. parallel folds/angle folds ("page-action pop-ups which move with the opening of the folded page, and require no additional user effort").

The Winder paper also independently reports Duncan Birmingham's competing framework: "true pop-ups" as extensions of the V-fold, the parallelogram, and/or the 45-degree fold — i.e., three primitive families rather than four, with wheels/pull-tabs excluded as "not true pop-ups" under Jackson & Forrester's stricter definition (a pop-up is "a self-erecting three-dimensional structure formed by the action of opening a crease" — explicitly excluding lift-flaps, pull tabs, and other 2D devices).

The Smithsonian brochure (see section 3 below) gives a fuller elaboration that functions as a superset/expansion of Carter & Diaz: it splits "movables" (flat-lying: volvelles, flaps, pull tabs) from "pop-ups" (rising: stage set/multiple layers, V-fold, box and cylinder, floating layers), then documents 13 named elements in detail.

Construction essentials extracted from practitioner sources (PaperPaul, HMG Pop Up, Nuffield Design & Technology guide — all WebFetched directly, treat as UNVERIFIED-but-fetched, i.e. real pages read once, not cross-checked):
- V-fold: glue tabs under backing sheet with the V's point on the central fold; angle of V determines lean (90deg=vertical, <90deg=leans back, >90deg=leans forward).
- Box/cylinder: score+cut a net, glue end tab to form a tube, glue lid tabs (inside=neater, outside=stronger), fold base tabs inward and V-fold-glue to backing.
- Platforms: non-moving platforms that ride the centerfold's motion, used to build layered/tall structures; can be locked with horizontal supports or left free-moving.
- Pull tabs: sliding tab/ribbon/string, can also trigger a pop-up; "waterfall" is a chained variant where multiple flaps open sequentially off one tab.

2. MULTI-PIECE ASSEMBLY ANATOMY (SABUDA/REINHART ERA)
===================================================================

Hard piece-counts are scarce in the literature — publishers don't publish teardown counts, and I could not access a real teardown article/video with numbers. What I found:

UNVERIFIED (search-snippet only; direct NPR fetch failed twice with connection resets, so treat as secondhand): For Sabuda's "Alice's Adventures in Wonderland: A Pop-up Adaptation" (2003), one spread depicts Alice caught in a whirlwind of playing cards — reportedly two full decks, 104 cards, each accurate to suit and royalty. The book's overall structure: six large spreads, each with one large pop-up plus a narrow booklet of several pages containing text and additional smaller pop-ups (this "large pop-up + booklet of secondary pop-ups" pattern recurs across Sabuda/Reinhart books per multiple secondary sources — e.g. Encyclopedia Prehistorica: Dinosaurs is described the same way: "six spreads with one spectacular large pop-up each, plus booklets of smaller pop-ups and text," with reviewers noting "so many pieces of paper make up each animal" that the publisher doesn't recommend it for small children without adult supervision opening pockets).

VERIFIED structural principle (from Winder et al. 2009, which explicitly analyzes a Sabuda mechanism, citing Sabuda's "America the Beautiful" 2004 as reference [5], with Fig. 1 showing "A pop-up mechanism from Sabuda, shown in three stages of opening"): the paper formalizes exactly how commercial multi-piece centerpieces are structurally organized — as SERIES and PARALLEL combinations of simple mechanisms:
- Parallel combination: a secondary mechanism shares a link/joint with the ground (base page) — most commonly, "the fold between a link and the ground becomes the gutter for another mechanism," letting a secondary mechanism be actuated by the same primary motion. Their explicit named example of this is the FLOATING LAYER (see section 3).
- Series combination: the secondary mechanism shares no attachment to the ground; it's driven entirely through the primary mechanism (examples given: "solid shapes" and 45deg folds, both becoming spatial mechanisms when combined this way).
- The paper states plainly: "many elaborate examples of layered mechanisms... the majority are combinations of V-folds, tents, tube straps, and arches." One-piece techniques are commonly used as SECONDARY mechanisms riding on layered PRIMARY mechanisms, because "when the base pages are fully open, the layered mechanisms can create the 90deg positions so favorable for one-piece designs."
- Complexity is also added by deforming link shapes without changing the underlying kinematics: "links may be curved, partially cut away, extended beyond their joints... This is among the most common ways of creating complexity in pop-ups and representing objects more realistically" — i.e., a Sabuda dragon's silhouette detail is mostly decorative link-shape variation layered onto a small number of kinematically simple underlying mechanisms, not fundamentally novel joint types.

So: structurally, a Sabuda/Reinhart "dimensional" centerpiece is best modeled as a SMALL number of kinematic primitives (V-folds, tents, box-folds) — combined in parallel (riding a shared crease) and in series (mechanism-driving-mechanism) — dressed with many decoratively-shaped, non-kinematic patches glued to the moving links. The "hundreds of pieces" figure so often quoted almost certainly refers to these decorative/illustrated patches (individually painted, cut, and glued), not hundreds of independent moving mechanisms.

3. FLOATING LAYERS / MULTI-TIER PLATFORMS
===================================================================

This is the best-documented mechanism of the six areas, and independent sources agree it is extremely common.

VERIFIED, Smithsonian Institution Libraries exhibition brochure "Paper Engineering: Fold, Pull, Pop & Turn" (2010-2011, National Museum of American History; curated by Stephen Van Dyk, Cooper-Hewitt, with Ann Montanaro, director of The Movable Book Society), full text read:

"Floating layers or platforms: This mechanism is best understood when seen from the side. Hinged multi-tier paper supports lift an illustration off the page, creating the illusion that it is floating over the surface."

The brochure explicitly lists "floating layers" as one of only FOUR basic pop-up construction techniques used across the entire history of the medium (the other three: stage set, V-fold, box and cylinder) — meaning it's not a niche technique but one of the four fundamental families.

VERIFIED, Winder/Magleby/Howell 2009 (full text read): formally defines "floating layer" as the canonical example of a PARALLEL mechanism combination (Fig. 20 in the paper, with a rigid-body/PRBM diagram): a secondary mechanism (e.g. another four-bar) has a joint attached to the ground exactly where the primary mechanism's ground-attached joint is, so the secondary mechanism's own "gutter" is created by the primary mechanism's motion — letting the secondary ride the primary's opening angle rather than needing independent actuation.

VERIFIED (WebFetch, read once), Nuffield Secondary Design & Technology "Pop-ups Design Guide" (practitioner instructional PDF), gives the concrete construction recipe:
- Single layer: cut a strip of card with glue tabs, fold, position with paper clips checking parts stay parallel to one page or the other, mark and glue.
- Multiple layers: "Cut two or three strips of card of different lengths... Hold in position with paper clips. Check that the layers are parallel and that the pop-up will not show when the card is closed... Glue the pop-up designs onto the individual layer mechanisms." — i.e., 2-3 tiers is the practitioner-guide's typical count, each a separate parallelogram-strip mechanism at a different height, all glued to the same backing.
- Floating layers (explicitly distinguished from "layered mechanisms"): "This mechanism uses a similar construction technique to multiple-layer pop-ups. The layers are positioned to support a design that spans the page, increasing the impression of depth" — i.e. floating layers = multiple parallel-strip tiers whose TOP surfaces are bridged by ONE continuous art piece spanning across all of them, rather than each tier carrying independent art. This is the key structural distinction from generic "layered mechanisms."

VERIFIED (WebFetch, read once), Eni Oken's pop-up mechanism sampler booklet: independently describes "Floating Layers with Pedestals: Uses three support structures of equal dimensions to elevate a layer above the base... the secret is to create all three pedestals the same size." This confirms a THREE-STRUT tripod as a common concrete implementation (3 non-collinear struts give a flat, non-wobbling platform).

VERIFIED, Carter & Diaz framework as reported by PaperPaul (WebFetch, read once): lists "Platforms" as a named category distinct from the moving mechanisms: "Non-moving platforms matching centerfold movement, creating layered or tall structures. Can be 'locked' with horizontal supports or intentionally left moveable." Also lists "V-fold Raisers": "Two V-folds that provide a structure to attach a platform to. Height increases upward movement but may reduce stability" — i.e., V-folds (not just parallelogram strips) are also used as the supporting struts under a floating platform.

Strut geometry / tier counts, synthesized across sources: typical real-book implementations use 2-3 tiers (Nuffield: "two or three strips"; Eni Oken: three pedestals), with each tier being either (a) a planar four-bar/parallelogram strip (Winder's "tent" — requires L1+L2=L3+L4 to fold flat) or (b) a spherical four-bar V-fold (PaperPaul's "V-fold raiser"), and the defining feature of "floating" specifically (vs. generic layering) is that ONE art piece is glued across the tops of MULTIPLE struts, spanning the gap between them, rather than each strut/tier carrying its own separate art.

4. ANGLE FOLDS AND MULTI-PLANE HERO PIECES
===================================================================

VERIFIED, Winder/Magleby/Howell 2009 (full text read): "Simple pop-ups which employ spherical mechanisms have joint axes which all intersect at a point on the gutter. Carter and Diaz call these mechanisms angle folds." The two canonical spherical mechanisms are single-slit/spherical-double-slit (one-piece, cut directly into the card) and V-folds (layered, glued tabs). Formally: ALL planes in a true angle-fold assembly share ONE common axis-intersection point on the spine — this is the defining geometric constraint, and it's exactly what the engine's existing symmetric/asymmetric v-fold (spherical four-bar, apex on spine) already implements for the 4-patch case.

UNVERIFIED (search-snippet only, from a page explicitly labeled as "Extract from Pop-Up Design and Paper Mechanics by Duncan Birmingham"): Birmingham's "M-fold" is described as effectively a triple V-fold made from ONE piece of card, raising an array of SIX planes and NINE gullies from a single sheet, all extendable or usable to mount further pop-up shapes. If accurate, this is the clearest documented example of a genuinely multi-plane single-assembly hero piece (6 distinct planes from one folded/cut sheet, all still sharing the spine-intersection constraint of an angle fold).

VERIFIED, Ruiz/Le/Yu/Low 2014 (Computer Graphics Forum — full text read): gives the FORMAL multi-plane construction for their "box-fold" — 11 labeled patches (G, B, L, R, FL, FR, BL, BR, TG, TB, C) arranged so L/R form equal angles with ground/backdrop, C is a perpendicular backbone glued at the G/B common fold, and TG/TB (top patches) are glued to C at their own common fold — i.e., a single box-fold assembly already comprises up to 7 distinct planar faces plus optional extra FL/FR/BL/BR "wing" patches, and the paper explicitly notes "each side is allowed to have multiple pairs of (FL,FR) or (BL,BR), as long as their fold lines do not intersect" — meaning the box-fold as already formalized supports an arbitrary number of additional coplanar-with-C wing patches, giving more faceting essentially for free within the existing math.

5. PULL-TABS, SLIDERS, AND SLIT-GUIDED MOTION
===================================================================

VERIFIED, Winder/Magleby/Howell 2009 (full text read) — the most rigorous formal treatment found:
- Sliders (prismatic joints): 1-DOF translation. "The layered nature of pop-up books lends itself quite well to the creation of sliding joints, because little extra is needed to completely constrain a joint for one degree-of-freedom translation. While the motion of a sliding joint is often within the plane of the page, it can be used to create out-of-plane motion by linking it to a pop-up mechanism. Many pull-tab mechanisms employ sliders."
- Rotating sliders (a "half-joint," 2-DOF): "allow one rotational and one translational degree of freedom... created with a slot through which a paper link moves... these joints do not restrict motion to the plane of the page, and their out-of-plane motion is often purposely hidden to create special effects."
- A "tube strap" (their term, citing Carter/Diaz) is the standard way a pull-tab or slider on one base page can DRIVE a mechanism mounted on the OTHER base page: the paper is doubled into a flattened rigid tube for compression/tension strength, kinematically equivalent to a "driver dyad" (an open kinematic chain) — it always appears combined with another mechanism, never alone.
- Structurally important for the engine: pull-tab/slider position is a function of an INDEPENDENT scalar input (the reader's drag distance s), NOT of the page dihedral angle theta. This matches Jackson & Forrester's definition explicitly excluding pull-tabs from "true pop-ups" — pull-tabs are categorically a second, orthogonal input channel.

VERIFIED (WebFetch, read once), Nuffield guide, concrete build recipe for "the sliding mechanism": cut a slot in the backing sheet along the desired travel direction; fold two small tab-pieces of card in half and pass them through the slot; cut a stiff pull-tab strip sized to the card; glue it to the back of the two small tabs; attach a guide near the card edge to constrain the tab; glue the driven image to the front of the two small pieces. Separately (search synthesis, UNVERIFIED-secondhand): "a simple way to guide the pull-strip is to weave it through three parallel slits in the page," and pull-strips "should be made double thickness... scored along its length, then folded over, stuck back to back" to resist buckling.

VERIFIED, Winder/Magleby/Howell 2009 also documents novel spatial extensions relevant to a future interactive phase: a "paper cylindric joint" (1 rotational + 1 translational DOF, axes collinear) and a "paper spherical joint" (3 rotational DOF, made from a single strip folded three times at right angles), with two demonstration mechanisms: a "circular arch" (spherical slider-crank: a pivot-mounted slider whose motion is constrained to a circle on the base page, driving a strip that lifts off the page as the slider is rotated by hand) and a "figure-8" (a paper RSSR spatial four-bar).

6. PROPORTIONS AND VISUAL CUES
===================================================================

The weakest-documented area — no rigorous, quantified rule of thumb found (no height-to-page ratio, no layer-spacing number), despite dedicated searches. Found:

- Nuffield guide (design philosophy): "Remember that too much decoration may distract from the three-dimensional image. A pop-up can look impressive left quite plain" — practitioners explicitly identify ORNAMENT as something that can undercut the read of dimensionality; the volumetric read comes from the fold geometry itself.
- Smithsonian brochure's framing of floating layers — "best understood when seen from the side" — implies the tell-tale visual signature of multi-tier dimensionality is a SIDE-ON silhouette showing stepped/staggered heights, i.e. an oblique or 3/4 viewing angle, since straight-on hides the tier gaps.
- makepopupcards.com practitioner tutorial (WebFetch, read once): identifies negative space/cutouts as contributing "to the sense of depth and space" beyond the raw pop-up geometry, and notes shadow-casting silhouettes can be deliberately manipulated — the only concrete rendering cue found: shadow management between tiers, not just geometry.
- Formal constraint: both Abel/Demaine et al. ("Algorithms for Designing Pop-Up Cards," STACS 2013, verified) and Li/Ju/Gu/Hu 2011 (verified) formalize "the closed paper is contained within the page border" as a required validity condition — "how tall can a hero piece be" is answered by folding math, not a fixed ratio.
- Could not verify numeric layer-spacing guidance from Carol Barton's "Pocket Paper Engineer" volumes or any teardown-photography article. Treat the absence of a hard number as a real gap.

RANKED RECOMMENDATIONS (with math sketches)
===================================================================

1. FLOATING MULTI-TIER PLATFORMS (tripod-of-identical-struts) — HIGHEST PRIORITY
Independently flagged by both an authoritative museum source and a peer-reviewed kinematics paper as one of only four fundamental pop-up families, and purely a COMPOSITION over existing primitives — no new solver needed.
Math sketch: pick N>=3 non-collinear instances of an existing primitive (planar four-bar tent or spherical V-fold), all with IDENTICAL link lengths/angles, placed at different positions along the spine. Every strut shares the same closed-form solution P(theta), so all N strut tips reach the same height/orientation at the same theta simultaneously — a flat rigid child plate glued across the tips has a single well-defined pose, still a pure closed-form function of theta (a tripod parallel mechanism degenerating to 1-DOF because all legs share one input). The defining visual: ONE art piece spans across the gap between tiers — likely the single biggest visual win.

2. RECURSIVE MECHANISM-ON-MECHANISM (box-fold/step-fold mounting) — SECOND PRIORITY
Directly answers what makes a Sabuda castle read as dimensional: mechanisms mounted on mechanisms, not more exotic single mechanisms.
Math sketch: Ruiz formalizes placement: "a tent-fold, v-fold and box-fold can only be positioned along the central fold or between TG and TB of a box-fold. Step-fold can only lie between a base patch and patch L or R." Since TG/TB already have closed-form poses in theta, treat {TG, TB} as a local page pair with derived local dihedral phi_child(theta) = angle-between(TG(theta), TB(theta)), then recursively invoke the EXISTING v-fold/box-fold/tent solver with phi_child as the driving angle — generalizing the Glassner-generations idea from "parent crease" to "any parent mechanism's derived local dihedral." Ruiz also gives non-intersection guards (x-range exclusion zones) for automatic validation.

3. MULTI-PLANE ANGLE-FOLD FAN (M-fold-style) — THIRD PRIORITY
Cheapest to implement; targets "reads as a piece of paper" directly — several coupled facets sharing one spine apex read as a sculptural volume.
Math sketch: instantiate k>=2 spherical-four-bar v-folds at the SAME (or closely adjacent) apex point on the spine with distinct angular parameters (alpha_i, beta_i). Each facet's plane-normal is independently closed-form in theta; because all axes intersect at the shared apex (spherical-mechanism guarantee), adjoining edges stay coincident automatically — a coherent multi-faceted solid (Birmingham's reported six planes / nine gullies from one sheet) via parameter-sweeping the existing v-fold primitive.
